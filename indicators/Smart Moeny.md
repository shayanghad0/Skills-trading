Here is a comprehensive, focused breakdown of **Smart Money liquidity** — what it is, its types, and exactly how to identify it on a price chart for trading purposes.

---

## 1. What Is Liquidity in Smart Money Concepts (SMC)?

In SMC, **liquidity** refers to the clusters of resting orders (stop-losses, pending buy/sell orders) placed by retail traders and breakout traders. Smart money — institutions, banks, market makers — needs liquidity to execute their large positions. They cannot simply buy or sell at any price; they need enough opposing orders to fill their massive transactions without slipping the price too much.

So they engineer price moves to **hunt liquidity**, triggering the orders of the losing crowd, using those triggered orders as fuel to enter their own positions in the opposite direction.

In simple terms:
- **Liquidity = the fuel for the market.**
- It sits where traders’ stops are naturally placed or where pending orders accumulate.
- Smart money moves price into these pools, absorbs the liquidity, and then reverses or continues the trend with momentum.

---

## 2. The Two Main Types of Liquidity (Must Know)

### A. Buy-side Liquidity (BSL)
- Orders that sit **above** significant highs.
- These are predominantly **sell-side stop-losses** of traders who are short, and **buy-stop orders** of breakout traders.
- Once price trades above these highs, the stops are hit (shorts cover = buy orders), and breakout traders get trapped. The smart money then uses that buy pressure to sell into, often causing a reversal.

### B. Sell-side Liquidity (SSL)
- Orders that sit **below** significant lows.
- These are **buy-side stop-losses** of long traders and **sell-stop orders** of breakout traders.
- When price drops below these lows, stops are triggered (longs liquidate = sell orders) and breakout sellers get in. Smart money uses that sell pressure to buy, often causing a reversal upward.

## 3. How to Identify Liquidity on a Chart (Step-by-Step)

Liquidity is identified by locating obvious structural points where retail traders would place their stops or pending orders. Here is exactly where to look:

### 3.1. Equal Highs / Equal Lows (Double Tops/Bottoms)
- **Equal Highs:** Two or more swing highs at the exact same price level. Traders put stops just above these highs thinking it's resistance. That resting liquidity above is buy-side liquidity.
- **Equal Lows:** Two or more swing lows at the same level. Stops accumulate below these lows. That's sell-side liquidity below.
- **How to spot:** Scan left on the chart. When you see a swing high that matches a previous swing high almost exactly, that zone is a liquidity pool. Smart money will often sweep that level (wicks above/below) to grab those orders.

### 3.2. Previous Session Highs and Lows (Daily/Weekly/Monthly Opens)
- **Previous day high & low:** High liquidity areas because many intraday traders use these as reference points for stops.
- **Previous week high & low:** Even larger liquidity pools.
- **Asian session high/low, London high/low:** In intraday forex/indices, the ranges of specific sessions act as liquidity magnets.
- **How to identify:** Mark the high and low of the prior daily candle or prior week's candle. The wicks or candle bodies are your liquidity levels. Often, price will raid one side (e.g., London takes out Asia high) before reversing.

### 3.3. Swing Highs and Swing Lows (Market Structure Points)
- **Swing High:** A candle high that is higher than the high of the candle before and after it.
- **Swing Low:** A candle low that is lower than the low of the candle before and after it.
- These are visible on any timeframe. Each swing point holds liquidity because traders place stops beyond them. The more obvious the swing point, the stronger the liquidity.
- **How to identify:** Use a zigzag indicator or visually mark every pivot. Stops sit just above recent swing highs (BSL) and just below recent swing lows (SSL). Smart money targets these in the direction of the current trend to continue the move (liquidity grab for continuation) or against the trend for a reversal.

### 3.4. Trendline Liquidity
- When price forms a trendline with multiple touches, stops collect just below an ascending trendline (for longs) and just above a descending trendline (for shorts).
- The longer and more obvious the trendline, the bigger the liquidity pool.
- **How to identify:** Draw valid trendlines connecting at least 2-3 swing points. The area just beyond each touch is the stop zone. A break of the trendline usually sweeps those stops before price reverses or accelerates.

### 3.5. Support and Resistance Levels (Horizontal)
- Classic horizontal S/R zones that have been respected multiple times accumulate orders.
- Below a strong support: sell-side liquidity.
- Above a strong resistance: buy-side liquidity.
- **Key:** The more times a level has been touched without breaking, the more stops sit beyond it, and the higher the probability of a liquidity grab.

### 3.6. High-Volume Nodes / High-Impact News
- While not always visible on candlesticks, areas where price consolidated for a long time hold a lot of trapped traders' stops just outside the range.
- **Consolidation ranges:** Mark the range high and low. Liquidity sits above the range high and below the range low. An aggressive break of either side often sweeps one of these pools before reversing.
- **How to identify:** Look for sideways price action (rectangle) with at least 5-6 candles. The high and low of that box are your liquidity magnets.

### 3.7. Psychological (Round) Numbers
- Levels like 1.1000, 1.1100, 1.2000 on EUR/USD or 100.00 on indices. Many pending orders and stops cluster around these whole and half numbers.
- **How to identify:** Simply note price approaching a major round number. Wicks beyond that number (e.g., 1.2005) often indicate a liquidity sweep.

## 4. Advanced Identification: Liquidity Types by Location (ICT Concepts)

For deeper understanding, SMC splits liquidity into external and internal, based on the dealing range or swing structure.

### External Range Liquidity (ERL)
- The highest high and the lowest low of a defined dealing range (like the daily or weekly range).
- **Buy-side ERL:** The highest high of that range.
- **Sell-side ERL:** The lowest low of that range.
- Price will often sweep the ERL before an expansion phase begins. Identify ERL by marking the absolute highs and lows of the previous leg or a trading range.

### Internal Range Liquidity (IRL)
- The nearest swing high or swing low *within* the current dealing range that hasn't been taken yet.
- If price is trending up, internal liquidity is the last intermediate swing high that could be swept for a retracement entry. If trending down, the last intermediate swing low.
- **How to identify:** In an uptrend, the most recent intermediate lower high that formed during a pullback is internal buy-side liquidity waiting to be grabbed before continuation. In a downtrend, the intermediate higher low is internal sell-side liquidity.

## 5. Practical Methods to Map Liquidity for Trading

Use this systematic routine:

1. **Start at the highest timeframe** (Daily/4H) and mark:
   - Previous week high and low.
   - Previous day high and low.
   - Major swing highs and lows.
   - Equal highs and equal lows that stand out.

2. **Drop to your execution timeframe** (1H/15min) and mark:
   - Asian session high and low (or overnight range).
   - London open range (for forex).
   - Current day's opening price (some models use midnight open liquidity).
   - Recent short-term swing points.

3. **Identify the "closest" liquidity pools:** 
   - If price is moving up, find the next high(s) above that haven't been tapped — these are buy-side targets.
   - If moving down, look for lows below — sell-side targets.

4. **Wait for a liquidity sweep:** Price must penetrate the level (typically a wick), triggering stops. The classic SMC trade enters *after* the sweep, looking for a reversal or a continuation with an order block entry.

## 6. Summary: Liquidity Identification Checklist

- [ ] Look for **equal highs / double tops** — resting buy-side liquidity above.
- [ ] Look for **equal lows / double bottoms** — resting sell-side liquidity below.
- [ ] Mark **previous day/week/session high and low** — stop hunts here are common.
- [ ] Identify **obvious swing highs and swing lows** — the more visible, the higher the liquidity.
- [ ] Find **trendline stops** — just below an uptrend line or above a downtrend line.
- [ ] Note **consolidation ranges** — liquidity above and below the range.
- [ ] Watch **round numbers** — wicks beyond them signal sweeps.
- [ ] Differentiate **external vs internal liquidity** for context on the larger move.

Once you master identifying where the orders are resting, you can align yourself with smart money by waiting for a liquidity grab, observing the market's reaction (displacement, change of character), and then entering in the opposite direction or in the trend’s continuation using an SMC entry model (order block, fair value gap, etc.).

This is exactly what smart money liquidity is and, critically, how to spot it on any chart for trading.
